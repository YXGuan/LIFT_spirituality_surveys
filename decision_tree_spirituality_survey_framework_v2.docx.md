**Decision-Tree Spirituality Survey Framework**

## 

| Purpose. This framework is for exploratory design, not for forcing a scripted conversation. The core idea is: start light if needed, triage the respondent’s current posture, then adjust the next question based on their worldview, openness, and level of familiarity with Christianity. |
| :---- |

# **Level Above the Main Branches: Ice Breaker Layer**

These questions run in parallel to the serious spiritual questions. They help reduce tension, build rapport, and give a natural bridge into deeper topics.

## **How to use this layer**

• If the setting feels cold, awkward, or rushed, start with 1–2 ice breakers before the spiritual triage question.

• If the respondent is already engaged and reflective, skip straight to the worldview triage question.

• Ice breakers can be funny, practical, or identity-based. Their job is not to be profound; their job is to open the person up.

| Category | Example questions | Comment on usage |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Funny / light | What is a very overrated thing everyone else seems to love?What is your most controversial food opinion?What’s the best worst campus food spot on campus? If you can rename a campus building, what would it be | Use when you need to lower tension quickly. |
| Practical / campus life | What program are you in, and what year?What has been the hardest part of this semester so far?What do you do to de-stress when school gets heavy? | Good default opener because it feels natural and situational. |
| Personal interests | What are you into outside school right now?What kind of content have you been consuming lately?What is something you have been excited about recently? | Useful if you want to humanize the interaction first. |
| Reflective but safe | What has been taking up most of your mental energy lately?What gives you hope when life feels heavy?What makes a week feel meaningful to you? | Good bridge from casual talk into deeper themes. |

# **Main Triage Question**

Use one broad classification question to decide the conversation path. Keep it simple and broad.

| Triage prompt: “are you spiritual? How would you describe your spirituality?” |
| :---- |

# **Main Branches (without sub-classification)**

## **1\. Atheist / non-belief**

| What this usually signals | The respondent likely values evidence, coherence, and honesty. They may also carry frustration with religion, or they may simply be unconvinced. |
| :---- | :---- |
| How the next follow-up should change | Do not rush into “Would you like to hear the gospel?” Start by understanding why they do not believe and what they think gives life meaning. The follow-up should be exploratory and respectful, not corrective. |
| Question options | • What led you to that view?• Do you think life has meaning or purpose without God?• How do you think morality works—objective, personal, or social?• Have you ever seriously explored Christianity, or did it never become persuasive to you? |
| Comments / cautions | • Avoid assuming they are angry at God; many are calm and thoughtful.• Avoid churchy language early.• A good next step is often Jesus as a historical or moral figure before moving to gospel language. |

## **2\. Agnostic / unsure**

| What this usually signals | The respondent is undecided, under-informed, conflicted, or open but unconvinced. |
| :---- | :---- |
| How the next follow-up should change | The next follow-up should clarify what keeps them uncertain: lack of evidence, too many religions, bad examples, or simply not enough thought. Keep the tone curious and invitational. |
| Question options | • What makes the question of God hard to answer for you?• Have you ever wanted to explore it more seriously?• If God did exist, what kind of God would make sense to you?• How open are you to hearing different perspectives? |
| Comments / cautions | • This branch often has the most openness.• Do not collapse “unsure” into “basically atheist.”• A gentle bridge into Jesus or Christianity can happen earlier here than with a firm atheist. |

## **3\. Spiritual but not religious**

| What this usually signals | The respondent may believe in transcendence, meaning, healing, energy, inner life, or personal spiritual practice, but may distrust institutions. |
| :---- | :---- |
| How the next follow-up should change | The next follow-up should connect with their spiritual vocabulary before introducing Christian categories. Explore whether they imagine a personal God, an impersonal force, or a self-directed spirituality. |
| Question options | • How would you describe your spirituality in your own words?• Do you think of the divine as personal, impersonal, or something else?• Where do your spiritual ideas mostly come from?• What comes to mind when you think about Jesus? |
| Comments / cautions | • Avoid framing religion as the obvious next step.• Institutional distrust may be stronger than theological resistance.• Use bridges such as meaning, longing, peace, forgiveness, identity, and hope. |

## **4\. Religious but not Christian**

| What this usually signals | The respondent already has a faith identity, community, and vocabulary for God or the sacred. |
| :---- | :---- |
| How the next follow-up should change | The follow-up should first honor that identity and ask them to describe it, rather than flattening all religions into the same bucket. Then explore their current understanding of Christianity and Jesus. |
| Question options | • What role does your faith play in your life right now?• What do you appreciate most about your tradition?• How familiar are you with Christianity?• Who do you understand Jesus to be? |
| Comments / cautions | • Do not turn the next question into debate mode too fast.• The aim at this stage is understanding and respectful contrast, not argument.• Keep comparison questions careful and non-combative. |

This category especially needs sub-routes because the next question should not be the same for a Muslim, a Jew, and a Buddhist.

| Sub-branch | Tailored questions |
| :---- | :---- |
| Jewish | What does being Jewish mean for you: religious, cultural, ethnic, or some combination? How do you think about God, covenant, and Scripture? What do you understand Christians to mean when they call Jesus the Messiah? |
| Muslim | How central is Islam in your daily life? What do you understand about Isa (Jesus) in Islam? How do you see the relationship between Allah, revelation, and previous scriptures? |
| Hindu | Is your practice more philosophical, devotional, temple-based, or cultural? How do you understand the divine: one reality, many expressions, many gods, or something else? |
| Buddhist | Is your approach more cultural, meditative, philosophical, or devotional? How do you think about suffering, desire, liberation, and whether ultimate reality is personal? |
| Sikh | How do you understand God, devotion, service, and the role of the Gurus? How familiar are you with Jesus beyond general respect for him? |
| Other / mixed tradition | Ask the person to describe the tradition in their own words before comparing it with Christianity. |

## **5\. Christian**

| What this usually signals | The respondent identifies as Christian, but the depth, clarity, and personal commitment may vary widely. |
| :---- | :---- |
| How the next follow-up should change | The next follow-up should distinguish between active faith, cultural identity, inherited label, and uncertain belief. Ask for their own understanding of Jesus and the gospel instead of assuming it. |
| Question options | • How would you describe your faith right now—active, cultural, inherited, or uncertain?• What does Jesus mean to you personally?• How would you explain the gospel in your own words?• How much does your faith shape your daily life? |
| Comments / cautions | • This branch is important because some who say “Christian” may have little actual understanding.• Avoid both flattery and suspicion; just clarify.• This path may lead either to encouragement or to a clearer presentation of the gospel. |

## **6\. Used to be Christian but not anymore**

| What this usually signals | The respondent likely has prior exposure, and may carry disappointment, pain, intellectual objections, or fatigue. |
| :---- | :---- |
| How the next follow-up should change | The next follow-up should focus on their story before content. Ask what changed, what they left, and what still feels unresolved. This branch often requires more empathy than explanation at first. |
| Question options | • What changed for you?• Was it more intellectual, personal, church-related, or gradual?• Are there parts of Christianity you still find meaningful?• What was your experience with Christians or church communities like? |
| Comments / cautions | • Do not assume they “just want to sin” or that they secretly still believe.• Many ex-Christians know Christian language well, so generic explanations can feel superficial.• Listening well here is more important than speed. |

## **7\. Not really thought about it / indifferent**

| What this usually signals | The respondent is not necessarily resistant; they may simply be busy, unengaged, or untouched by the topic. |
| :---- | :---- |
| How the next follow-up should change | The follow-up should be light and relevance-focused. Instead of abstract theology first, ask whether purpose, meaning, stress, suffering, or identity questions matter to them personally. |
| Question options | • Have questions about meaning or purpose ever felt important to you?• What tends to matter most to you day to day?• Do spiritual questions feel irrelevant, uninteresting, or just distant?• Would you be open to exploring them if it connected to real life? |
| Comments / cautions | • Do not overload this branch with heavy doctrinal questions too soon.• Concrete life questions work better than abstract apologetics.• This branch often benefits from strong ice-breaker use before triage. |

## **8\. Scientific / skeptical lens**

| What this usually signals | The respondent likely values evidence, method, critical thinking, and intellectual consistency. This may overlap with atheism or agnosticism, but should still be treated distinctly. |
| :---- | :---- |
| How the next follow-up should change | The next follow-up should explore how they see the relationship between science and faith, instead of assuming those are automatically opposed. This lets the conversation engage their actual framework. |
| Question options | • How do you see science and religion relating to each other?• Do you think science can address questions of meaning and morality, or only mechanism?• What kind of evidence would make spiritual claims more plausible to you?• Have you encountered thoughtful Christian perspectives on science? |
| Comments / cautions | • Do not present faith as anti-reason.• Avoid lazy “science cannot explain everything” statements unless handled carefully.• This branch often responds well to precise language and fewer clichés. |

## **9\. Ethics / society focused**

| What this usually signals | The respondent may care deeply about justice, compassion, service, community, inequality, or social healing, while seeing religion as secondary, mixed, or harmful. |
| :---- | :---- |
| How the next follow-up should change | The next follow-up should begin with moral concern and lived values. Explore where they think human dignity, justice, and moral obligation come from, and whether religion helps or harms those goals. |
| Question options | • What most shapes your concern for society or justice?• Where do you think human dignity comes from?• Do you see religion as more helpful or harmful in society?• Do you think moral progress needs something beyond social agreement? |
| Comments / cautions | • This branch is often more open to the practical and ethical teachings of Jesus than to religious systems.• Avoid sounding defensive about all religious failures.• A strong bridge here is the person of Jesus and the kind of kingdom he announced. |

# **Compact Notes for Later Web-App Implementation**

• Each branch can be represented as one node with metadata: branch label, follow-up guidance, example questions, and caution notes.

• The app does not need dozens of sub-branches. It only needs enough logic to select the next best question.

• A future implementation could store each branch as: worldview label → guidance text → recommended next questions → reconvergence target.
